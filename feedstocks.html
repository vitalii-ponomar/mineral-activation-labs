Feedstocks that matter

The feedstocks that matter most in low-carbon mineral development are not always the most obvious ones. What matters is the balance between reactivity, scale, processing demand, and realistic end use.

01

Clays

Clays represent one of the strongest long-term opportunities for low-carbon binders, particularly where calcination can convert locally available materials into useful cementitious components.

Kaolinitic clays Illitic clays Smectitic clays Mixed clay soils

Strong when

  • Clay mineralogy is favourable
  • Activation or calcination is feasible at scale
  • Local substitution is economically relevant

Often overestimated when

  • Mineralogy is poorly defined
  • Quartz dilution is high
  • Processing costs are ignored

Commercial role

Primary candidate for scalable SCM and regional binder strategies.

02

Soils

Soil-derived materials are rarely ideal from a pure chemistry perspective, but become highly relevant where disposal pressure, reuse, and local circularity drive value.

Excavated soils Treated soils Construction spoil Fine mineral fractions

Strong when

  • Large volumes require reuse
  • Regulatory pathways allow controlled application
  • Blending or stabilisation is sufficient

Often overestimated when

  • High-value binder roles are assumed
  • Contamination constraints are ignored
  • Variability is underestimated

Commercial role

Strong in circular, low-cement, and site-specific applications.

03

Slags

Slags remain benchmark industrial feedstocks, but the strongest opportunities are now selective rather than generic, depending on chemistry, structure, and availability.

Granulated slag Steel slag Copper slag Fe-rich slags

Strong when

  • Glass content and reactivity are high
  • Supply is consistent
  • Clear pathway fit exists

Often overestimated when

  • All slags are treated as equivalent
  • Volume stability issues are ignored
  • Route-specific compatibility is unclear

Commercial role

High-performance systems where quality and route are controlled.

04

Tailings

Tailings are scale-driven opportunities. Their value rarely comes from intrinsic reactivity, but from volume, site context, and pathway alignment.

Silicate tailings Iron ore tailings Lithium residues Beneficiation fines

Strong when

  • Large volumes justify development
  • Carbonation or low-grade applications are viable
  • Logistics align with local use

Often overestimated when

  • Reactivity is assumed without testing
  • Transport and handling are ignored
  • High-value applications are assumed

Commercial role

Large-volume, pathway-specific material strategies.

05

Fine mineral residues

Fine residues are rarely standalone high-value feedstocks, but can be highly useful where they solve blending, packing, or disposal problems.

Fly ash Kiln dusts Crusher fines Filter cakes Quarry residues

Strong when

  • Used as system components
  • Close to point of use
  • Performance contribution is sufficient

Often overestimated when

  • Treated as primary reactive feedstocks
  • Chemistry variability is ignored
  • Processing needs are underestimated

Commercial role

Pragmatic, system-level components rather than headline materials.

What separates strong feedstocks from weak ones

A useful feedstock is not simply the most reactive one. The stronger candidates usually combine a clear material role, a realistic processing route, sufficient scale, and a commercially sensible use case.

Clear material role

The feedstock must contribute meaningfully to a defined function.

Realistic processing pathway

The required processing must be technically and economically credible.

Scale and consistency

Volume and variability must align with real deployment.

Commercial fit

The pathway must solve a real industry problem, not just a technical curiosity.

Reactivity, scalability, and practicality

Most material decisions are not decided by reactivity alone. In practice, the most relevant comparison is across technical promise, scale, and how realistic the route is to actual use.

Feedstock questions

These are the questions that usually sit underneath early feedstock decisions, especially when a material appears promising on paper but its real development value is still uncertain.

Do you only work with waste materials?

No. Natural minerals, industrial residues, and underused side streams can all be relevant if they offer a credible low-carbon or circular-material opportunity.

Can low-grade clays still be useful?

Yes. Lower-grade materials can still be valuable when the activation route, processing intensity, and local economics are aligned.

Does a reactive material always make the best feedstock?

No. Highly reactive materials can still be unattractive if supply is limited, variability is high, or processing demand is too heavy.

Do contaminants always rule out reuse?

No. But they often narrow pathway options, increase testing burden, or shift the most realistic use case toward more controlled applications.

Assess a feedstock opportunity

Whether the material is a clay, slag, tailings stream, treated soil, or fine mineral residue, the key question is usually the same: is there a credible route from material availability to useful product value?
