Feedstock strategy

Strong mineral projects start with better feedstock selection.

Not every mineral stream deserves the same development effort. The goal is to identify which materials are genuinely attractive, which only work under specific conditions, and which should be redirected or dropped early.

The key feedstock questions
  • What is the material, really?Bulk chemistry alone rarely tells the full story.
  • Where can it win?The right product route matters more than a generic “low-carbon” label.
  • What could kill the opportunity?Processing demand, impurities, inconsistency, or weak market fit.
Core feedstock categories

The most relevant material classes are broad. The technical logic for each is not.

Different feedstocks carry different promises and different traps. Good qualification means recognising those differences early.

Clays

Clay-rich soils and clay resources

Useful in calcined clay routes, activated systems, stabilisation, and fired products when mineralogy and heat demand support the case.

Slags

Metallurgical and industrial slags

Potentially strong in activated systems and blended materials, but often constrained by phase complexity, sulphates, iron-rich fractions, or processing history.

Tailings

Mining residues and quarry fines

High-volume streams where scale can be attractive, but beneficiation burden and logistics often become decisive.

Soils

Excavated, treated, and fine earth materials

Often overlooked, but capable of fitting stabilisation, low-cement blends, or fired products when properly classified and processed.

Residues

Fine mineral residues and process-derived materials

Byproducts that may add real value as supplementary, filler, or blended components when composition and consistency are acceptable.

Carbonation feedstocks

Ca- and Mg-bearing mineral sources

Relevant to accelerated carbonation, mineral curing, and carbon-linked product concepts where reaction pathway matters as much as chemistry.

What actually controls value

A feedstock becomes attractive when technical fit and deployment fit line up.

Mineralogy and phase assemblageReactive phases, amorphous content, clay species, iron-bearing phases, and deleterious minerals define the real opportunity.
Chemistry and impuritiesMajor oxides matter, but salts, sulphates, organics, LOI, trace elements, and variability can matter more.
Processing burdenA feedstock that only works after intensive beneficiation may quickly lose its advantage.
Supply realityVolume, repeatability, location, moisture, and handling often determine whether a pathway is commercially credible.
IndicatorImplication
High reactive fractionImproves the case for SCM or activated routes.
Mostly inert mineralogyMay still be valuable in fill, filler, ceramic, or blended pathways.
Severe impurity burdenRaises risk for standards fit, durability, processing, or environmental acceptance.
Consistent large volumeMakes project translation much more believable for manufacturers and infrastructure users.
Early exclusion matters

Knowing what not to develop is part of strong technical strategy.

Feedstock qualification is not only about finding wins. It is also about stopping weak routes early and redirecting materials toward more realistic product categories.
