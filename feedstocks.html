Feedstocks

Core feedstock categories

We focus on mineral feedstocks that may support low-carbon construction materials and related mineral products. The key question is not whether a material is available, but whether it can support a credible technical and commercial pathway.

What usually decides feedstock value

ReactivityDoes the material actually contribute to useful phase formation or strength development?
Processing realismWill grinding, calcination, washing, or classification make the pathway uneconomic?
Supply realityCan it be sourced consistently at scale and in a location that makes sense?

Feedstocks we evaluate

Not every material belongs in the same product. Early evaluation helps determine whether a stream is best suited to SCM, activation, stabilisation, ceramics, carbonation, or no further development.

01

Clay-rich soils and clays

Often considered for calcined clay, low-cement blends, ceramics, or stabilisation. Mineral type and impurity profile matter more than bulk oxide chemistry alone.

02

Metallurgical slags

Can behave as useful binder precursors or remain technically difficult depending on phase assemblage, chemistry, cooling history, and activator compatibility.

03

Tailings and mineral residues

Need careful screening for inert dilution, variability, contaminant risk, and processing demand before higher-value applications are pursued.

04

Quarry fines and mixed mineral fines

May create value in lower-intensity pathways such as blended materials, controlled low-strength applications, or engineered fill.

05

Calcium- or magnesium-rich residues

Can open carbonation-related pathways where phase chemistry and reaction window support useful CO₂-reactive products.

06

Difficult secondary streams

Some materials are neither simple wastes nor obvious products. These are often the most commercially interesting if the mechanism can be resolved.

Reactivity, scalability, and practicality

A feedstock becomes valuable when mineralogy, chemistry, process demand, and likely performance align. Many streams fail not because they are unusable, but because the wrong pathway is selected too early.

  • Phase assemblage controls what is reactive and what is inert.
  • Chemistry shapes impurity risk, gel chemistry, and durability fit.
  • Processing determines whether technical promise survives commercial reality.

Typical feedstock questions

  • Is the feedstock worth developing at all?
  • Which application pathway is most technically credible?
  • What constraints will matter most before pilot scale?
  • What minimum testing set is needed to make the next decision?
