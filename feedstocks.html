Core feedstock categories

The page is intentionally focused on five feedstock groups that are most relevant to low-carbon construction materials and mineral valorisation work: clays, soils, slags, tailings, and fine mineral residues.

01

Clays

Clays are one of the clearest mineral feedstock opportunities for low-carbon binders, especially where activation or calcination can convert locally available materials into useful cementitious components.

Kaolinitic clays Illitic clays Smectitic clays Mixed clay soils

Where the opportunity sits

  • Calcined clay SCMs
  • Reactive aluminosilicate precursors
  • Blended cement systems
  • Local substitution strategies

What usually limits value

  • Mineralogical variability
  • Quartz dilution and impurities
  • Thermal activation sensitivity
  • Processing cost and consistency

Strategic note

Clays tend to sit at the strongest intersection of technical promise, regional relevance, and long-term scalability.

02

Soils

Soil-derived feedstocks are usually less ideal in a purely chemical sense than selected clays or slags, but they become important where reuse, disposal pressure, and local circularity create a strong practical case.

Excavated soils Treated soils Construction spoil Fine mineral fractions

Where the opportunity sits

  • Low-cement fill and flowable materials
  • Stabilised or activated soil-based systems
  • Blended mineral feedstocks
  • Higher-value reuse pathways

What usually limits value

  • Heterogeneity and moisture variability
  • Organics and contamination history
  • Regulatory constraints
  • Lower intrinsic reactivity

Strategic note

Soils are most compelling where practical reuse value matters more than maximum reactivity.

03

Slags

Slags remain benchmark industrial feedstocks for many cementitious and activated systems, although technical quality, route fit, and supply constraints differ strongly between slag types.

Granulated slag Steel slag Copper slag Fe-rich slags

Where the opportunity sits

  • Alkali-activated binders
  • SCM replacement and blended systems
  • High-performance matrices
  • Selected carbonation routes

What usually limits value

  • Crystalline versus amorphous balance
  • Expansion or instability risks
  • Residual metals or sulfur
  • Plant-to-plant variability

Strategic note

Slags often offer strong performance, but the best slag opportunities are no longer simply about availability—they are about selective, route-specific use.

04

Tailings

Tailings matter because of their scale. They are rarely simple drop-in feedstocks, but they can become important where very large volumes justify pathway-specific development.

Silicate tailings Iron ore tailings Lithium residues Beneficiation fines

Where the opportunity sits

  • Carbonation-reactive pathways
  • Supplementary mineral components
  • Engineered fill or binder blends
  • Large-volume regional material strategies

What usually limits value

  • Low reactivity in many systems
  • Residual reagents and contaminants
  • Water management and handling
  • Remote-site logistics

Strategic note

Tailings are most attractive when volume, site context, and pathway design align closely enough to overcome modest intrinsic value.

05

Fine mineral residues

Fine residues are often overlooked because they are not always highly reactive, yet they can still be commercially useful where modest performance contribution solves a real blending, packing, or disposal problem.

Fly ash Kiln dusts Crusher fines Filter cakes Quarry residues

Where the opportunity sits

  • Fine mineral blending
  • Filler and SCM hybrid roles
  • Packing optimisation
  • Local valorisation of overlooked by-products

What usually limits value

  • Inconsistent chemistry
  • Salts, carbon residues, or moisture
  • Limited fit without pre-treatment
  • Lower intrinsic reactivity

Strategic note

Fine residues are often strongest as practical system components rather than standalone headline feedstocks.

Reactivity, scalability, and practicality

A useful feedstock is not simply the most reactive one. The more meaningful comparison is usually across three questions at once: how strong is the technical potential, how scalable is the supply, and how practical is the route to real use.

Feedstock Technical promise Scalability Practicality Main advantage Main constraint
Kaolinitic clay High Medium–High Medium Strong reactivity pathway after activation Processing intensity and deposit quality
Illitic or mixed clay soil Medium High Medium Broad regional relevance More pathway-sensitive performance
Granulated slag-type feedstock High Medium High Strong benchmark performance Supply and strategic availability
Steel slag Medium Medium Medium Large industrial relevance Stability and route control
Mine tailings Low–Medium High Low–Medium Very large volume potential Low intrinsic value in many cases
Excavated soils Low–Medium High Medium Strong circularity and reuse relevance Variability and compliance constraints
Fine mineral residues Low–Medium Medium–High High Useful as pragmatic system components Often limited as standalone reactive feedstocks

Feedstock questions

A few common questions that usually come up when thinking about whether a material stream is worth pursuing.

Do you only work with waste materials?

No. The focus is broader than waste alone. Natural minerals, industrial residues, and underused side streams can all be relevant if they offer a credible low-carbon or circular-material opportunity.

Can low-grade clays still be useful?

Yes. Lower-grade materials can still be valuable when the target pathway, activation route, processing intensity, and local economics are aligned.

Does a reactive material always make the best feedstock?

Not necessarily. A highly reactive material may still be unattractive if supply is limited, variability is high, contaminants are problematic, or processing demand is too heavy.

Do contaminants always rule out reuse?

No. But contaminants can narrow pathway options, increase testing burden, or shift the most realistic use case toward more controlled applications.

Can variable materials still be commercially viable?

Sometimes yes. Variability can often be managed through screening, blending, quality windows, and pathway-specific specifications, provided the economics remain sensible.

Have a mineral feedstock in mind?

Whether the material is a clay, slag, tailings stream, treated soil, or fine mineral residue, the key question is usually the same: is there a credible route from material availability to useful product value?
