Feedstock categories

Different mineral feedstocks create different product pathways

Clays

Clay-rich materials can support calcined SCM routes, activated binder systems, filler strategies, ceramic products, or blended low-cement formulations. The right route depends on mineralogy, impurity profile, available processing, and the target performance level.

Kaolinitic clays Illitic clays Smectitic clays Mixed clay soils

Key route-shaping factors and likely commercial role for this feedstock group.

Where they fit well

  • Calcined SCM and low-clinker substitution strategies
  • Alkali-activated and hybrid binder systems
  • Ceramic or thermally processed products where firing already exists

What usually decides the route

  • Clay mineral assemblage and quartz dilution
  • Whether calcination, classification, or blending is realistic
  • Regional availability and price pressure on cement replacement

Commercial role

Strong candidate for scalable regional SCM supply and tailored low-carbon binder development.

Soils

Soil-derived materials are often application-led rather than chemistry-led. Their value can sit in controlled reuse, low-cement products, site-linked stabilisation, ceramic transformation, or blended systems where circularity and disposal avoidance matter.

Excavated soils Treated soils Construction spoil Fine mineral fractions

Key route-shaping factors and likely commercial role for this feedstock group.

Where they fit well

  • Low-cement fills, blocks, and stabilised products
  • Blended mineral systems with controlled processing
  • Reuse pathways driven by disposal cost and local circularity

What usually decides the route

  • Variability, contamination status, and compliance pathway
  • Whether screening, drying, blending, or firing is needed
  • Whether the application is site-specific or market-facing

Commercial role

Strong in circular, logistics-led, and disposal-offset applications where local material value is enough.

Slags

Some slags suit high-performance binder systems, others fit carbonation, aggregate, ceramic, or route-specific mineral product strategies. The opportunity is rarely generic. It depends on phase assemblage, glass content, expansion risk, and supply reality.

Granulated slag Steel slag Copper slag Fe-rich slags

Key route-shaping factors and likely commercial role for this feedstock group.

Where they fit well

  • Supplementary cementitious and activated binder systems
  • Carbonation-treated products and mineral processing routes
  • Performance-led applications where chemistry is controlled

What usually decides the route

  • Reactive glass content versus crystalline phases
  • Volume stability, free lime, MgO, and route compatibility
  • Consistency of supply and competitive market pull

Commercial role

High-performance option where feedstock quality is understood and a defined pathway can absorb it.

Tailings

Tailings can support carbonation routes, mineral fillers, aggregates, ceramics, geotechnical products, or selective binder strategies. Their value often comes from scale, local deployment, and risk reduction rather than from one inherently reactive chemistry.

Silicate tailings Iron ore tailings Lithium residues Beneficiation fines

Key route-shaping factors and likely commercial role for this feedstock group.

Where they fit well

  • Large-volume local products and mine-site reuse
  • Carbonation-led mineral utilisation strategies
  • Pathways where logistics and storage reduction drive value

What usually decides the route

  • Particle size, mineralogy, and residual processing chemistry
  • Transport distance and the practicality of local deployment
  • Whether value is created through volume absorption or upgrading

Commercial role

Large-volume, site-linked opportunity where pathway fit can outweigh low intrinsic margin per tonne.

Fine residues

Fine mineral residues can create value through packing, blending, controlled reactivity, process integration, ceramics, or logistics-led reuse. They do not need to be headline binder ingredients to become commercially relevant.

Fly ash Kiln dusts Crusher fines Filter cakes Quarry residues

Key route-shaping factors and likely commercial role for this feedstock group.

Where they fit well

  • Blended systems that benefit from filler or fine-particle effects
  • Products that can absorb variable secondary mineral inputs
  • Disposal-offset pathways close to the point of generation

What usually decides the route

  • Particle size distribution, moisture, and handling demand
  • Chemistry variability and how much control the product needs
  • Whether the value comes from performance, disposal, or both

Commercial role

Pragmatic feedstocks that strengthen overall material systems and solve process or disposal problems.

What defines the right pathway for a feedstock

Application fit

Start with the product role the material can serve well, whether reactive, filler, ceramic, carbonation-led, or site-specific.

Activation strategy

Processing, blending, calcination, carbonation, stabilisation, or beneficiation can shift the most realistic use case.

Supply reality

Volume, variability, moisture state, and location can matter as much as chemistry once a route is scaled.

Commercial pull

The strongest pathway is the one that solves a real logistics, cost, compliance, or performance problem for industry.
