Feedstocks

Mineral feedstocks for low-carbon materials

This page focuses on the feedstocks themselves: what types of mineral resources matter, where the opportunities usually sit, and what technical constraints commonly define their usefulness.

01

Mineralogy

Phase assemblage controls reactivity, phase evolution, and compatibility with activation or supplementary cementitious use.

02

Chemistry

Chemistry shapes binder formation, impurity risks, durability, environmental fit, and standards relevance.

03

Processing demand

Grinding, calcination, classification, drying, or washing can decide whether a feedstock is commercially realistic.

04

Supply reality

Volume, consistency, location, and logistics often matter as much as laboratory reactivity.

Core feedstock categories

We focus on feedstocks that repeatedly appear in low-carbon construction and mineral valorisation work: clay-rich materials, soils, slags, tailings, and fine mineral residues. The aim here is not to list every possible stream, but to frame the main groups that are most relevant in practice.

01

Clays

Clays are among the most promising mineral feedstocks for low-carbon binders and supplementary cementitious systems, but their value depends strongly on clay type, impurity content, thermal activation response, and local processing feasibility.

Kaolinitic clays Illitic clays Smectitic clays Mixed clay soils Low-grade deposits

Where the opportunity sits

  • Calcined clay SCMs
  • Reactive aluminosilicate precursors
  • Blended cement systems
  • Region-specific clay activation strategies

What usually limits value

  • Variable mineralogy and quartz dilution
  • High iron, alkalis, or other impurities
  • Non-ideal calcination response
  • Deposit heterogeneity and moisture handling

Why this category matters

Clays offer one of the clearest pathways toward local, scalable mineral substitutions where conventional SCM supply is constrained.

02

Soils and excavated materials

Excavated soils and treated soils are often seen primarily as disposal problems, yet selected fractions can become useful mineral resources when mineralogy, contamination status, and processing route are properly understood.

Excavated soils Treated soils Construction spoil Fine mineral fractions

Where the opportunity sits

  • Low-cement fill and flowable materials
  • Stabilised or activated soil-based systems
  • Blended mineral feedstocks
  • Higher-value reuse of difficult spoil streams

What usually limits value

  • Heterogeneity and variable moisture
  • Organics and contaminants
  • Regulatory constraints
  • Low intrinsic reactivity in some fractions

Why this category matters

Soils are abundant, highly relevant to infrastructure and remediation work, and often important where circularity and landfill reduction are key drivers.

03

Slags and metallurgical residues

Slags remain one of the most technically important industrial feedstocks for cementitious and activated systems, but performance depends on glass content, phase assemblage, chemistry, fineness, and route-specific compatibility.

Blast furnace slag Steel slag Copper slag Fe-rich slags

Where the opportunity sits

  • Alkali-activated binders
  • SCM replacement and blended systems
  • High-performance or chemically resistant matrices
  • Carbonation-based valorisation in selected streams

What usually limits value

  • Crystalline versus amorphous balance
  • Volume instability in some slags
  • Metallic residues, sulfur, or trace contaminants
  • Plant-to-plant variability

Why this category matters

Slags are still among the strongest benchmarks for reactive industrial mineral feedstocks, even as supply and quality become more constrained.

04

Mine tailings

Mine tailings are increasingly important as both an environmental liability and a large-scale mineral resource. Their usefulness depends on mineralogy, particle size, residual process chemistry, storage condition, and intended application.

Silicate tailings Iron ore tailings Lithium residues Beneficiation fines

Where the opportunity sits

  • Supplementary mineral components
  • Carbonation-reactive pathways
  • Engineered fill or binder blends in selected cases
  • Large-volume regional material strategies

What usually limits value

  • Low reactivity in many systems
  • Residual reagents and trace contaminants
  • Water management and handling
  • Remote-site logistics and social scrutiny

Why this category matters

Tailings matter because of their scale. Even modest technical value can become important when very large volumes are available.

05

Fine mineral residues

A wide range of ashes, quarry fines, kiln dusts, filter cakes, and other fine mineral residues can offer value where chemistry, particle size, and consistency align with a practical product route.

Fly ash Kiln dusts Crusher fines Filter cakes Quarry residues

Where the opportunity sits

  • Fine mineral blending
  • Filler and SCM hybrid roles
  • Packing optimisation
  • Local valorisation of overlooked by-products

What usually limits value

  • Inconsistent chemistry
  • Salts, carbon residues, or moisture
  • Declining availability of some traditional ash streams
  • Limited fit without pre-treatment

Why this category matters

These streams are often close to existing operations and can be commercially attractive when a modest performance contribution solves a real disposal or supply problem.

Feedstock questions

A few common questions that usually come up when thinking about whether a material stream is worth pursuing.

Do you only work with waste materials?

No. The focus is broader than waste alone. Natural minerals, industrial residues, and underused side streams can all be relevant if they offer a credible low-carbon or circular-material opportunity.

Can low-grade clays still be useful?

Yes. Lower-grade materials can still be valuable when the target pathway, activation route, processing intensity, and local economics are aligned.

Does a reactive material always make the best feedstock?

Not necessarily. A highly reactive material may still be unattractive if supply is limited, variability is high, contaminants are problematic, or processing demand is too heavy.

Do contaminants always rule out reuse?

No. But contaminants can narrow pathway options, increase testing burden, or shift the most realistic use case toward more controlled applications.

Can variable materials still be commercially viable?

Sometimes yes. Variability can often be managed through screening, blending, quality windows, and pathway-specific specifications, provided the economics remain sensible.

Have a mineral feedstock in mind?

Whether the material is a clay, slag, tailings stream, treated soil, or fine mineral residue, the key question is usually the same: is there a credible route from material availability to useful product value?
