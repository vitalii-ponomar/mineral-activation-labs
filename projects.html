Soils

Treated soils for low-cement binder pathways

Australia · treated soil reuse
Reuse qualification

The challenge

Large treated and excavated soil streams are generated with limited high-value reuse options. The core question is whether these materials can support low-cement products or should be ruled out early.

What we did

Assessed mineralogy, chemistry, impurity constraints, and reactivity, then tested realistic pathways including calcination, blending, and alternative binder use.

What became clear

Some soil streams showed credible potential as reactive binder components. Others were constrained by low clay reactivity, quartz dilution, or contaminant-related limitations.

Why it matters

This helps distinguish between soils worth developing and soils unlikely to justify further processing or pilot effort.

Slags

Fe-rich slags for low-carbon binder development

Finland · steel and slag valorisation
Binder development

The challenge

Fe-rich slags are often treated as difficult or inconsistent feedstocks. The commercial question is whether they can support reliable binder performance and acceptable environmental behaviour.

What we did

Evaluated phase assemblage, activator response, microstructure development, and leaching behaviour under practical alkali-activation conditions.

What became clear

Fe-rich slags are not simply problematic by-products. Under the right chemistry, they can function as viable binder precursors, but performance depends strongly on feedstock chemistry and process control.

Why it matters

This supports more confident decisions around slag development, product positioning, and whether further pilot work is justified.

Residues

Secondary residues for carbonation-based products

Europe · processing and scale-up context
Processing route

The challenge

Many secondary mineral residues are abundant but difficult to use in conventional cementitious systems. The key issue is whether processing can unlock enough value to justify development.

What we did

Developed and evaluated carbonation–hydration routes while tracking phase evolution, microstructure, and resulting material performance.

What became clear

Combined processing can improve phase stability and create more useful binding behaviour, but only within specific processing windows. Not every residue is a strong candidate.

Why it matters

This turns broad waste-reuse ambition into a clearer go / no-go position on processing-led product development.

Clays

Calcined clays for SCM feedstock qualification

Australia · clay reactivity and qualification
SCM qualification

The challenge

Calcined clays are attracting strong market interest, but not all clay-rich materials behave the same way. A major risk is assuming feedstocks are interchangeable.

What we did

Combined advanced and laboratory characterisation to examine how mineral type, structural change, and calcination response influence downstream reactivity.

What became clear

Feedstock selection matters. Clay mineral type, structural disorder, and thermal response all influence whether a material is likely to perform as a useful SCM.

Why it matters

This supports more disciplined clay selection before larger processing, product-development, or pilot-stage commitments are made.

Ceramics

Excavated soils for ceramic and brick products

Australia · alternative product pathway
Ceramic pathway

The challenge

Excavated soils are widely available, but their suitability for ceramic products depends on mineral composition, contaminant behaviour, and firing response.

What we did

Evaluated chemistry, mineralogy, firing behaviour, and material evolution during thermal treatment, with attention to both technical and environmental constraints.

What became clear

Some soil streams can be directed toward ceramic products through controlled formulation and firing, while others carry composition or emissions risks that limit practicality.

Why it matters

This provides a more realistic basis for deciding whether ceramic product development is viable for a given soil stream.
